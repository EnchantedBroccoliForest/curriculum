## Key ideas

- Instruction tuning (SFT) is maximum likelihood on (prompt, response) pairs with the loss masked to response tokens; rejection sampling fine-tuning samples $n$ responses, keeps the best by a reward model, and runs SFT on the survivors.
- PPO: with ratio $\rho_t = \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid s_t)$ and advantage $\hat A_t$, the surrogate is $\mathbb{E}\big[\min(\rho_t \hat A_t,\; \mathrm{clip}(\rho_t, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat A_t)\big]$. Clipping removes the incentive to move $\rho_t$ outside $[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$ in the direction the advantage rewards.
- PPO for language models needs a value model as baseline; GRPO replaces it with a group of samples per prompt and normalises rewards within the group ([week 34](/week/34) derives the estimator).
- LoRA: a weight update $\Delta W = BA$ with $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$, $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$; trainable parameters per adapted matrix are $r(d+k)$. Training memory saves the gradient and optimizer bytes of the frozen weights, not the activation memory, and the frozen weights still have to be resident (QLoRA stores them quantised).
- The fine-tuning frameworks (TRL, Unsloth, Axolotl) differ in API and speed, not in objective; the arithmetic and judgment do not change between releases.

## Common misconceptions

- "PPO's clip is a learning-rate trick" → It changes the objective so that moving the ratio past the clip range in the rewarded direction earns nothing.
- "LoRA makes fine-tuning cheap in every respect" → It cuts trainable parameters and optimizer state; forward and backward FLOPs through the frozen weights remain.
- "GRPO is a different objective from PPO" → It is a different advantage estimator for the same KL-regularised objective.
