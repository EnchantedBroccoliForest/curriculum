# Week 16: Prince ch. 20: why does deep learning work?

## Why this week

Scaling laws are the quantitative claim a founder most often meets in a technical report or a vendor pitch, and the one most often overfit. This week reads the two papers that set the convention (Kaplan 2020, Chinchilla 2022) alongside Prince's chapter on what is and is not understood about why deep networks generalize. Week 22 fits an IsoFLOP frontier by hand; weeks 19 and 25 reconstruct training budgets that lean on these fits.

## Objectives

- Write the Kaplan and Chinchilla parametric forms and state which variables each holds fixed and which it varies.
- List the assumptions behind a power-law fit of loss against compute and name a concrete way each can fail.
- Produce paper notes for both papers in the one-paragraph format: claim, evidence, weakest point, judgment questions failed.

## Key ideas

- The basic observation: on a fixed data distribution, test loss $L$ falls as a power law in parameters $N$, data $D$ and compute $C$ over several orders of magnitude, $L(x) \approx (x_0/x)^{\alpha_x}$, with small exponents.
- Chinchilla's parametric form fits $L(N, D) = E + A/N^{\alpha} + B/D^{\beta}$; the compute-optimal allocation under $C \approx 6ND$ follows by minimizing $L$ subject to that constraint. The $6ND$ approximation itself is a FLOP count you should be able to reproduce in week 18.
- Kaplan and Chinchilla disagree on the optimal $N$ versus $D$ split. The difference traces to experimental choices (learning-rate schedule, counting of embedding parameters, range of $D$), not to a change in the underlying law; this is the week's main lesson about fits.
- Three fitting methods appear in Chinchilla (fixed $N$ with varied $D$, IsoFLOP profiles, parametric fit); they agree roughly, and the IsoFLOP method is the one you reproduce in week 22.
- The irreducible term $E$ is an estimate of the entropy of the data distribution under the tokenizer; a fitted $E$ is not a measurement of it.
- Extrapolation risk: a power law fitted on $10^{18}$–$10^{21}$ FLOPs says nothing about where it bends; the "curve" in a plot is a fit, not data, past the last point.
- Prince ch. 20 surveys the generalization question (over-parameterization, implicit regularization, the lottery-ticket and double-descent pictures) and is honest that none of it is a theory of why scaling works; treat its claims as questions.
- Downstream metrics (benchmark accuracy) are not what the laws fit; emergence claims on such metrics depend on the metric's nonlinearity in the loss, which is a construct-validity question.

## Common misconceptions

- "Scaling laws are laws." → They are empirical fits with an extrapolation range equal to the measured range; no mechanism predicts the exponents.
- "Chinchilla overturned Kaplan." → Chinchilla changed the optimal allocation estimate, mostly by fixing the schedule confound; the power-law form and the broad picture are shared.
- "Lower loss means better downstream performance." → Loss on the pretraining distribution and downstream task metrics are related monotonically only in aggregate; individual task metrics can be flat or non-monotone over a range.
